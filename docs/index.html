Abstract

Content that once ranked and drew clicks quietly loses traction over time, and by the time a decline is obvious in a dashboard, weeks of visibility are already gone. This study asks whether a page's next-month decline in search clicks can be predicted from its current-month Google Search Console signals, so a content team can review the right pages before the drop compounds.

Using 134,238 page-months of real, anonymized FlyRank search data, we built a logistic regression model on five signals — impressions, clicks, average position, CTR, and CTR-gap-to-position-benchmark — trained on February 2026 data to predict March 2026 decline, validated with a client-grouped train/test split. The model reached a precision of 0.552 and an AUC of 0.900, against a single-variable baseline rule that scored only 0.065 precision and 0.256 AUC — worse than the page-level decline rate itself (18.2%).

The result is a ranked, reason-coded list of pages that most warrant editorial review, plus a concrete lesson: an intuitive-sounding heuristic — "low CTR relative to position means trouble" — does not, on its own, predict which pages are about to decline.

1. Problem Statement

Content decay is a persistent, quiet problem in search visibility. Pages that once performed well gradually lose clicks due to algorithm shifts, competitor movement, staleness, or simple neglect — and this loss is rarely obvious until it has already compounded over several reporting cycles. A content team with limited review capacity needs a way to prioritize which pages to check first, rather than reviewing all pages equally or waiting for a visible traffic collapse.

This project supports exactly that decision: given a page's current search performance, which pages are worth a reviewer's time this month, and why? The output is not a claim about causation — it does not say why a page is declining — but a decision-support ranking that directs limited attention where it is most likely to matter.

2. Data

  • Source: the FlyRank ML Internship dataset (hf://datasets/FlyRank/internship-warehouse), accessed via a gated Hugging Face token.
  • Table: fact_content_daily_performance, a daily grain table (one row = one page, one day, one client), aggregated here to page-month.
  • Time window: features built entirely from February 2026 (month=2026-02). The decline label compares February clicks to March clicks — features never see the outcome month.
  • Filtering: restricted to rows where client_has_gsc IS TRUE and gsc_data_available IS TRUE, and to pages with at least one recorded impression in February.
  • Excluded: the ai_* referral breakdown columns were excluded from modeling — sparsely populated in this panel, and not needed for a click-decline signal specifically.
  • Final analysis set: 134,238 page-months, of which 18.2% declined and 81.8% did not.

3. Methodology

3.1 Features

  • total_impressions — total February GSC impressions.
  • total_clicks — total February GSC clicks.
  • avg_position — average February search position.
  • ctr — February click-through rate.
  • ctr_gap — the page's CTR subtracted from the average CTR of pages in the same position bucket (1–3, 4–10, 11–20, 21–50, 51+). Positive means underperforming position peers.

Every feature is computed from data fully observed at the moment a reviewer would act — nothing is derived from March, and nothing is derived from the label itself.

3.2 Label Definition

declining = 1 if a page's total March 2026 clicks were strictly lower than its total February 2026 clicks; 0 otherwise. This is a proxy for real content decay, not a ground-truth judgment — a single month-over-month comparison can also reflect ordinary noise, seasonality, or a one-off event.

3.3 Baseline

The baseline mirrors a hand-written rule built earlier in this track: flag a page as "declining" if its ctr_gap falls in the worst 20% of the training set — treating CTR underperformance relative to position as a stand-in for decline risk.

3.4 Validation Design

Train/test split: GroupShuffleSplit (70/30), grouped by client_hash_id — entire clients, not individual pages, are assigned to either train or test, so the model never trains and tests on different pages from the same client.

Model: LogisticRegression with class_weight='balanced', on standardized features (zero mean, unit variance). Standardization was necessary because raw feature scales differ by several orders of magnitude — without it, predicted probabilities saturated toward 0 or 1 for nearly every page regardless of actual risk, making the ranking meaningless. This was caught and corrected during development — see Limitations.

5. Limitations

  • Multicollinearity: ctr_gap is derived directly from ctr, so the two features compete for the same signal. Their coefficients are large and opposite in sign, making individual coefficients harder to interpret in isolation — even though overall predictive performance (AUC 0.900) is stable.
  • Feature scale sensitivity: an early, unscaled version of this model produced predicted probabilities pinned at 1.0 for nearly all pages, driven by impressions' raw magnitude dominating the logit. Standardizing features corrected this — reported here as a limitation of logistic regression on unscaled inputs generally, not a property of the data.
  • Low-volume noise: pages with very few impressions can produce extreme CTR ratios (1 click / 1 impression = 100% CTR) that aren't reliable signal. A minimum-impression threshold (100+) was applied before ranking, though two rows in the final top 10 (108 and 242 impressions) remain close to that floor and warrant lower confidence.
  • Proxy label, single comparison window: the decline label compares exactly two months and cannot distinguish a genuine structural decline from ordinary noise, seasonality, or a one-off event. A page flagged as "declining" is observed to have fewer March clicks than February clicks — nothing more.
  • Directional support, not causal proof: this model identifies pages statistically similar to others that lost clicks. It does not identify why a given page is losing clicks, and makes no claim about Google's ranking algorithm or the causal effect of any specific content change.
